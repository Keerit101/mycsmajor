Laptops

Your laptop runs an editor, a browser, a terminal and maybe a few containers. Almost any modern machine can do that. Spend on the parts you will feel every day.

What matters

  • 16 GB of RAM or more. This is the spec that ages worst. Browsers, IDEs and Docker all want memory.
  • 512 GB SSD. Toolchains, virtual machines and datasets add up fast.
  • All-day battery. Lecture halls rarely have enough outlets.
  • A good keyboard and screen. You will stare at both for four years.

What you can skip

A gaming GPU. Unless you plan to train machine learning models locally, coursework runs fine on integrated graphics, and many universities give you access to lab or cloud GPUs.

Best all-rounder

MacBook Air (Apple silicon)

  • Excellent battery life and silent, fanless design
  • Unix-based, so the terminal works like most servers
  • Choose 16 GB of RAM or more

Keyboards

An external keyboard is the cheapest upgrade for long study sessions, especially if you raise your laptop screen to eye level.

What matters

  • Layout. Tenkeyless or 75% boards keep your mouse closer, which is easier on your shoulder.
  • Noise. Loud clicky switches are fun at home and unwelcome in a library or shared room.
  • Multi-device switching. Handy if you move between a laptop, tablet and desktop.

Headphones

Good headphones turn a noisy dorm, bus or lab into a place where you can focus, and a decent microphone matters for group calls and remote interviews.

What matters

  • Active noise cancelling if you study in busy places.
  • Comfort for multi-hour sessions. Over-ear is usually easier on long days than earbuds.
  • Microphone quality for calls, especially internship interviews.

On a tight budget?

  • Check your university's student discount and refurbished stores before buying new.
  • A used business laptop with 16 GB of RAM and an SSD is a great Linux machine.
  • Put money into RAM first. You can add a keyboard and headphones later.
